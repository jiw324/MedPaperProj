\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into electronic health record (EHR) systems for clinical summarization, yet their security properties in realistic EHR workflows remain underexplored. Prior work evaluates LLM robustness largely in isolation, without systematically studying models operating over mixed-trust EHR inputs that combine verified clinical facts with untrusted patient-provided instructions. We introduce the benchmark for evaluating generation-time semantic integrity of LLMs in an EHR system setting. Using synthetic patient records, we simulate a realistic EHR summarization pipeline and evaluate six widely used LLMs under clean and adversarial conditions. Focusing on safety-critical facts such as documented allergies, we find that adversarial patient instructions can reliably induce fact omission despite explicit access to ground-truth records: all six commonly used LLMs in modern EHR systems exhibit attack success rates near or above 90\%. We further evaluate a trust-anchoring defense that redundantly injects verified EHR facts into the generation context; while this defense does not prevent attacks, it significantly reduces silent failures by forcing conflicts to become explicit. These results demonstrate that current LLMs lack robust trust-boundary enforcement in EHR-integrated settings and motivate standardized benchmarks and defenses tailored to clinical deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems are the core digital platforms used in modern healthcare to document, store, retrieve, and exchange patient information across clinical settings. They maintain structured data such as diagnoses, medications, allergies, laboratory results, vital signs, and immunizations, alongside unstructured content including clinician notes, discharge summaries, imaging reports, and patient portal messages. EHRs support routine clinical workflows such as documentation, order entry, care coordination, and controlled access to patient records by authorized clinicians and staff within and across organizations \citep{onc_ehr_benefits,onc_playbook_ehr,wang2023ehr_source}.

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into EHR systems to assist with clinical documentation tasks, including chart summarization, handoff notes, and provider-facing summaries \citep{woo2025scoping,bednarczyk2025summarization,aalijamia2025bhc}. In these workflows, LLMs often operate over \emph{mixed-trust inputs}, combining structured, clinician-verified patient records with unstructured patient-reported text originating from portals or messaging systems. Although the underlying EHR data may remain accurate and uncompromised, the model’s generated output can nonetheless become clinically incorrect if untrusted instructions override verified facts during generation.

This integration gives rise to a subtle but safety-critical failure mode that is not well captured by existing evaluations: \emph{semantic integrity violations at generation time}. Unlike database tampering or system compromise, these failures occur entirely within the model’s instruction-following behavior when it is presented with conflicting trusted and untrusted inputs. Recent clinical AI evaluations have raised concerns about factual inaccuracies and reliability in LLM-generated clinical documentation, but often without isolating the mechanisms by which such errors arise \citep{chung2025nejmai,croxford2025evaluating}.

Prior benchmarks for clinical LLMs have primarily emphasized task performance, such as question answering accuracy, summarization quality, or clinical reasoning, typically using EHR-derived corpora and benchmark datasets \citep{kweon2024ehrnoteqa,aalijamia2025bhc}. These evaluations generally assume a uniform trust level across model inputs and do not explicitly assess whether verified clinical facts are preserved in the presence of untrusted instructions. Consequently, they offer limited insight into model behavior under realistic EHR integration settings, where trust boundaries exist implicitly rather than being formally enforced.

In this work, we introduce a benchmark for evaluating semantic integrity in EHR-integrated LLMs under mixed-trust inputs. The benchmark formalizes a simple but realistic workflow: (1) the model is provided with structured ground-truth patient data representing clinician-verified EHR facts; (2) an untrusted instruction is introduced; (3) the model is tasked with generating a clinical summary; and (4) the output is compared against the ground truth to identify integrity violations. We consider both clean and adversarial conditions to isolate the effect of untrusted instruction dominance, closely related to prompt-injection--style vulnerabilities described in prior LLM security guidance \citep{owasp_prompt_injection}. % AI-SUGGESTION: Unlike classic prompt injection that targets privileged model instructions, our setting is driven by the EHR integration layer mixing untrusted portal text with trusted facts, leading to silent omission/negation of verified items without modifying system prompts or using tools.

Using this framework, we evaluate multiple frontier and open-weight LLMs on chart summarization tasks involving allergies and medications. We quantify integrity failures using automatic metrics that capture both fact omission and explicit contradiction, and analyze how these behaviors vary across models. We further evaluate a trust-anchored integrity-check defense that enforces explicit separation between trusted EHR facts and untrusted patient-reported information---not as a complete mitigation, but as a diagnostic mechanism for surfacing otherwise silent errors.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We introduce a benchmark that explicitly models mixed-trust EHR workflows, enabling systematic evaluation of semantic integrity violations in LLM-generated clinical summaries.
    \item We show that untrusted instructions can induce clinically meaningful omissions and contradictions across multiple models, even when underlying EHR data remains correct.
    \item We demonstrate that enforcing explicit trust separation and integrity checks can reduce or expose integrity failures, transforming silent errors into inspectable violations.
\end{enumerate}

By isolating generation-time semantic integrity failures from infrastructure compromise and training-time attacks, this benchmark provides a focused perspective on a critical yet under-evaluated risk in real-world clinical LLM deployment.

\section{Background and Problem Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

Modern EHR systems store heterogeneous clinical information, including both structured fields (e.g., diagnoses, medications, allergies, vital signs, and laboratory measurements) and unstructured narrative content (e.g., clinician notes, discharge summaries, and imaging reports) \citep{ehrenstein2019obtaining,kim2023challenges}. In parallel, many health systems support patient-facing portals that enable patients to access portions of their record and communicate with care teams through secure messaging \citep{onc_secure_messages,ahrq_secure_messaging}. As a result, routine clinical workflows frequently involve reviewing and synthesizing information that originates from different documentation channels and differs in format, granularity, and verification status.

In practice, EHR interfaces and surrounding applications often present clinicians with curated views that combine structured record elements with free-text documentation and message threads. This mixed presentation is particularly common in documentation workflows, where clinicians reconcile discrete chart facts with contextual narrative text and recent communications. The resulting record is used for ongoing care coordination, clinical decision-making, and longitudinal documentation \citep{wang2023ehr_source}.
We refer to this EHR-integrated workflow throughout the paper; \Cref{fig:ehr_system} provides the corresponding high-level system context for our mixed-trust threat model.

\paragraph{Threat Model.}
We consider an adversary who can submit \emph{untrusted free-text content} via a patient-facing channel (e.g., patient-portal secure messages or similar text that is ingested into the summarization context) that may be included verbatim alongside clinician-verified EHR facts.
Concretely, this capability can arise either because the attacker manipulates/tricks the patient into submitting the adversarial content, or because the attacker compromises/stole patient credentials and submits messages directly via the portal.
The attacker’s goal is to induce \emph{generation-time semantic integrity violations} in the provider-facing summary---specifically, omission or contradiction of safety-critical chart facts such as allergies and active medications.
The attacker may send a single message or multiple messages over time and can choose message timing within the normal workflow, but cannot directly modify structured EHR fields, clinician-entered problem lists, or the underlying database.
We assume no access to system prompts, no tool use, and no privileged retrieval beyond what the standard EHR interface places into the model context.
The system goal is to produce a concise chart summary that preserves clinician-verified facts even when untrusted text is present; failures matter because clinicians may rely on the summary during time-sensitive decision-making.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLMs for Clinical Documentation}

Large language models have been increasingly explored for clinical documentation tasks, including note drafting, chart summarization, and discharge or handoff summaries \citep{woo2025scoping,bednarczyk2025summarization}. Recent work has introduced datasets and evaluation protocols targeting longitudinal hospital-course and discharge-style summarization, enabling systematic comparison of model outputs on clinically grounded tasks \citep{aali2024mimicivbhc}. These efforts primarily assess linguistic quality, clinical coverage, and usefulness of generated summaries, but generally assume that all model inputs are equally trusted and do not evaluate behavior under conflicting or untrusted instructions.

\subsection{Clinical Benchmarks for EHR-Centered Language Tasks}

A substantial body of prior work has developed benchmarks for clinical natural language processing and EHR-centered language understanding, including question answering over clinical notes and structured records \citep{pampari2018emrqa}. More recent benchmarks extend these efforts to multi-document and EHR-derived tasks, such as question answering over discharge summaries and longitudinal records \citep{kweon2024ehrnoteqa}. While these benchmarks capture important aspects of clinical reasoning and information retrieval, they typically evaluate task performance under a single trust assumption and do not explicitly test whether verified clinical facts are preserved when models are exposed to adversarial or untrusted inputs.

\subsection{Factuality and Reliability of Clinical LLM Outputs}

Concerns about factual correctness and reliability of LLM-generated clinical text have motivated evaluations focused on hallucinations, omissions, and factual verification in medical documents \citep{chung2025nejmai}. These studies highlight the risk of silent errors in generated clinical content, particularly in safety-critical settings. Our work is complementary to this line of research: rather than studying factuality in aggregate, we focus on a specific failure mode arising from system integration—generation-time semantic integrity violations—where explicitly provided, clinician-verified facts are omitted or contradicted due to the influence of untrusted instructions.

\subsection{Prompt Injection and Mixed-Trust Inputs}

Prompt injection has been widely recognized as a fundamental vulnerability in LLM-based systems, stemming from the lack of robust separation between instructions and untrusted data \citep{owasp_prompt_injection}. Although most prior work studies prompt injection in general-purpose or agentic settings, the underlying mechanism is directly relevant to EHR-integrated workflows, where patient-reported text, system instructions, and structured clinical facts are often combined within a single context. Our work adapts this security perspective to clinical documentation by explicitly evaluating whether untrusted inputs can override verified EHR facts during generation.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

In contrast to prior clinical LLM benchmarks and evaluations, our work explicitly models mixed-trust EHR workflows and evaluates semantic integrity under clean, adversarial, and defended conditions. Rather than measuring task accuracy alone, we quantify whether LLM-generated clinical summaries preserve clinician-verified ground-truth facts when exposed to untrusted instructions. This benchmark complements existing clinical summarization and question-answering benchmarks by isolating an integration-driven failure mode rooted in instruction-following behavior, enabling controlled comparisons across models and interface-level defenses.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Benchmark Construction from Synthetic EHR Data}

We construct our benchmark using synthetic electronic health record (EHR) data generated by Synthea, leveraging its tabular exports to obtain structured, clinician-verified patient information.
Synthetic data enables controlled experimentation without exposing real patient health information while preserving realistic EHR structure.

We derive two task-specific cohorts:
\textit{(i)} allergy summarization and
\textit{(ii)} medication summarization.
For each task, we sample up to $N=1000$ patients who meet the corresponding inclusion criterion of having at least one documented allergy or at least one active medication.
Sampling is performed deterministically using a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility.

For each task, we persist a fixed benchmark subset consisting of:
an identifier list (see Appendix~\ref{app:file-patient-ids}),
a patient demographics table (see Appendix~\ref{app:file-patients-subset}),
and the corresponding fact table for the task (allergies: Appendix~\ref{app:file-allergies-subset}; medications: Appendix~\ref{app:file-medications-subset}).
All experiments reuse these frozen subsets across models and conditions.

\subsection{Task Definition}

Each benchmark instance pairs structured chart facts with an untrusted patient-reported message.
The structured chart facts represent clinician-verified EHR data and consist solely of allergy or medication entries.
The untrusted patient message simulates patient portal input and is not guaranteed to be correct.

The task is to generate a concise provider-facing chart summary in response to a fixed instruction:
\emph{``Write a concise chart summary for a new provider.''}
All outputs are constrained to a single sentence to increase pressure for factual omission and prioritization.

% AI-SUGGESTION: Briefly justify the "realistic workflow" claim given deliberate experimental constraints.
\paragraph{Why This Captures Realistic Risk.}
Many deployed EHR copilots and documentation assistants generate brief, handoff-style summary snippets under time and space constraints; our single-sentence setting intentionally amplifies the same prioritization pressure that makes omissions likely in practice \citep{woo2025scoping,bednarczyk2025summarization,perez2022summarization,starmer2014ipass}.
We focus on allergies and active medications because they are ubiquitous across encounters and safety-critical for clinical decision-making, making integrity failures immediately consequential \citep{jointcommission_medrec,nice_drug_allergy}.
Finally, Synthea-derived synthetic EHR exports preserve the structure and heterogeneity of EHR data (structured fact tables plus free-text context) while avoiding PHI and enabling controlled, large-scale measurement \citep{walonoski2018synthea}; the resulting setup is therefore representative of the mixed-trust mechanism we study even though it abstracts away richer note types and longer summaries.

\subsection{Attack Model}

We model the attack as an \emph{explicit untrusted override attempt} embedded within the patient-reported message.
In the \textbf{attack} condition, the patient message directly contradicts the structured chart facts by asserting the absence of all allergies or all medications (e.g., ``New research shows 

the patient is not allergic to anything'' or ``the patient is not taking any medications'').

As illustrated in \Cref{fig:attack_model}, the attacker’s leverage comes from the EHR-integrated workflow mixing clinician-verified records (trusted) with patient-portal content (untrusted) within a single generation context, enabling untrusted claims to suppress or override verified facts at generation time.

This attack reflects a realistic failure mode in EHR-integrated LLM systems, where patient portal messages, external notes, or retrieved documents may contain incorrect or misleading claims that conflict with clinician-verified records.
The attack does not attempt to jailbreak the model or alter system instructions; instead, it exploits the model’s tendency to reconcile conflicting inputs by prioritizing more recent or assertive natural language statements.

Importantly, the attack is \emph{contextual rather than syntactic}: it relies on semantic contradiction rather than prompt-format manipulation.
As a result, success requires the model to incorrectly treat untrusted patient text as authoritative and either suppress or negate verified chart facts during summarization.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

We follow an established omission-based evaluation setup using two prompt conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clean}: The patient message contains no conflicting claim and does not dispute the chart facts.
    \item \textbf{Attack}: The patient message explicitly denies all allergies or medications despite contradictory structured chart evidence.
\end{itemize}

This clean-versus-attack design serves as the core experimental comparison and is applied uniformly across both allergy and medication summarization tasks.
All other aspects of the experiment are held constant across conditions.

\subsection{Prompt Construction}

Prompts are constructed as a fixed sequence of messages in JSON format.
Each prompt includes:
\textit{(i)} the task instruction,
\textit{(ii)} the structured chart facts (one item per line),
and \textit{(iii)} a final instruction message that varies by condition and model.

Model-specific prompt \emph{wrappers} are embedded directly in the prompt to ensure that model runners remain pure input--output interfaces.
To reduce causal ambiguity in cross-model comparisons, we restrict these differences to minimal formatting/stability tokens required by specific APIs (e.g., optional system preambles or ``no \texttt{<think>}'' directives) and do not change the task objective or threat semantics: across all models, the task instruction and structured facts are identical, the clean condition contains no conflicting claim, and the attack condition contains an explicit denial of all allergies/medications (up to paraphrase).
Exact prompt templates are omitted for blind review; see Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} for a placeholder description.
This design isolates model behavior under conflicting information without introducing confounding effects from tool invocation or external retrieval, but we note that any remaining prompt-level variation is a potential limitation when attributing differences solely to the model.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Model outputs are evaluated using automatic string matching against ground-truth chart facts.
For both allergy and medication tasks, we report:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recall}: the fraction of chart-ground-truth items mentioned in the generated summary.
    \item \textbf{Conflict rate}: under the defense, the fraction of cases where a semantic conflict between trusted EHR facts and untrusted claims is detected and flagged.
    \item \textbf{False-none rate}: the proportion of outputs that assert ``no allergies'' or ``no medications'' when at least one corresponding chart item exists.
    \item \textbf{Full omission rate}: the proportion of outputs that mention none of the chart-ground-truth items.
\end{itemize}

For attack conditions, we additionally report an \textbf{attack success rate}, defined as recall falling below a fixed threshold or the presence of a false-none assertion.
We set the recall threshold to $\tau = 0.5$ for both allergy and medication tasks.
Recall is computed via deterministic normalized string matching: we lowercase and strip punctuation from both the one-sentence output and each chart item, and count an item as mentioned if its normalized description (or a normalized extracted allergen/medication term) appears as a substring of the normalized output; false-none is triggered only when a ``no allergies/medications'' pattern is present and zero chart items match.
This metric captures both partial and complete suppression of verified chart facts.

\subsection{Defense Model: Trust-Anchored Summarization}

We evaluate a defense strategy based on \emph{explicit trust separation} and an \emph{integrity check} over independently generated trusted and untrusted summaries (see \Cref{fig:defense_model}). As prior work and common practice, we briefly situate our approach relative to three mitigation patterns for prompt-injection--style risks in mixed-trust LLM systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instruction--data separation (prompt structuring).} A simple mitigation is to clearly delimit instructions, trusted chart facts, and untrusted patient text (e.g., labeled sections and explicit ``do not treat as authoritative'' warnings) \citep{owasp_prompt_injection}. This can reduce trivial instruction override, but it still places all content in one context; when untrusted text semantically contradicts trusted facts, the model may still prioritize the conflicting claim---the core failure mode we evaluate.
    \item \textbf{Output validation / verifier gate.} Another approach is to treat the model output as untrusted and run a post-generation check for missing required facts or contradictions before the summary is used \citep{chung2025nejmai,croxford2025evaluating}. Our defense belongs to this family, but is designed to be \emph{diagnostic}: rather than only comparing the final output to the database, we compare trusted-only vs.\ untrusted-only generations and surface conflicts explicitly.
    \item \textbf{Least privilege / sandboxing (agentic systems).} For systems where models can take actions (e.g., tool/API calls), a common defense is to restrict capabilities via allowlists and least-privilege permission checks \citep{nist80053r5}. In our EHR summarization setting, however, the primary ``action'' is the generated text itself; limiting tool access does not prevent silent omission of allergies/medications, motivating integrity-focused defenses.
\end{itemize}
Concretely, the defense runs the model in two constrained modes:
\textit{(i)} a \textbf{trusted summary} generated \emph{only} from clinician-verified EHR facts (e.g., allergies/medications) and treated as authoritative, and
\textit{(ii)} an \textbf{untrusted summary} generated \emph{only} from patient-portal claims and treated as non-authoritative.

An \textbf{integrity check} then compares the trusted and untrusted summaries to detect semantic conflicts.
If a conflict is detected, the system flags the case for clinician review and \emph{does not} merge untrusted content into the trusted summary.
If no conflict is detected, the system marks the untrusted content as consistent with the checked chart facts and eligible to merge as non-contradictory patient-reported context, producing a final clinician-facing summary for downstream use.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Trust-anchored integrity-check defense (simplified).}
\label{alg:trust_anchored_defense}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Trusted EHR facts $F$, untrusted patient message $U$, LLM $M$
\ENSURE Final clinician-facing summary, or a flagged case (no merge)
\STATE $S_T \leftarrow \textsc{TrustedSummary}(M, F)$
\STATE $S_U \leftarrow \textsc{UntrustedSummary}(M, U)$
\STATE $C \leftarrow \textsc{IntegrityCheck}(M, F, S_T, U, S_U)$ \COMMENT{$C$ contains \texttt{CONFLICTS\_TO\_VERIFY} (CTV)}
\IF{$\textsc{ParseError}(C)$}
  \STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{FlagForReview}($S_T$) \COMMENT{conservative default; do not merge}
\ENDIF
\STATE $\mathit{conflicts} \leftarrow \textsc{ExtractCTVOrNONE}(C)$
\IF{$\mathit{conflicts} \neq \text{NONE}$}
  \STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{FlagForReview}($S_T$, $\mathit{conflicts}$) \COMMENT{do not merge}
\ENDIF
\STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{MergeAsContext}($S_T$, $S_U$) \COMMENT{trusted facts take precedence}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Integrity Check Implementation.}
In our implementation, the integrity check is rule-based and operates on an \emph{inspectable output format} enforced by the defense prompt: the model must emit labeled sections \texttt{SUMMARY\_TRUSTED}, \texttt{UNTRUSTED\_NOTE}, and a bullet list under \texttt{CONFLICTS\_TO\_VERIFY}.
We flag a conflict if \texttt{CONFLICTS\_TO\_VERIFY} contains any non-empty bullet other than normalized variants of ``none''/``no conflicts.'' 
If the expected headers or bullets are missing (formatting mismatch / parse error), we conservatively treat the case as a failure of the defense pipeline in our evaluation (and count it as an attack success under attack).
This checker does not run separate entailment or LLM-as-judge inference; instead, it measures whether the model explicitly surfaced a conflict in the designated section (including hedged/uncertain phrasing, if present).

We evaluate this defense under clean and attack conditions, measuring (a) whether trusted EHR facts are preserved (fact retention / recall) and (b) whether conflicts are explicitly detected and surfaced.
This defense is intended to reduce \emph{silent} integrity failures in EHR-integrated LLM systems by making trust boundaries and disagreement explicit at generation time, rather than assuming a single mixed-trust context can be safely summarized.

\section{Future Work}

This work focuses on generation-time semantic integrity failures arising from mixed-trust \emph{textual} inputs in EHR-integrated LLM systems. While this setting captures a common deployment scenario today, several important directions remain open.

\paragraph{Multimodal Attack Surfaces in Clinical Workflows.}
EHR-adjacent patient communication channels increasingly support richer media (e.g., photos and videos) alongside secure messages, which can later be ingested by downstream clinical workflows and AI systems \citep{onc_secure_messaging}. As LLM-based systems evolve toward multimodal models that jointly process text, images, and video, these modalities introduce new mixed-trust attack surfaces. Extending semantic-integrity benchmarks to multimodal EHR inputs—where trusted structured data must remain authoritative despite conflicting visual evidence—represents a critical next step.

\paragraph{Image-Based Prompt Injection and Visual Fact Suppression.}
Recent research shows that vision-language models can be manipulated via \emph{visual prompt injection}, including images containing embedded text instructions or adversarially constructed visual content that changes the model’s behavior \citep{bagdasaryan2023multimodal, clusmann2025vlm_injection}. In an EHR context, analogous attacks could involve scanned documents or patient-uploaded images asserting the absence of allergies/medications, potentially overriding structured chart data when summarized by multimodal models. Future work should formalize image-based omission and contradiction attacks in EHR workflows and evaluate whether visual inputs can induce the same class of generation-time semantic integrity violations observed in our text-only setting.

\paragraph{Video and Temporal Manipulation Attacks.}
Patient-uploaded videos add temporal structure and narrative framing that may implicitly contradict clinician-verified records (e.g., apparent non-adherence or symptom resolution). As multimodal models begin to summarize longitudinal patient context from mixed media, it will be important to assess whether temporal visual narratives can bias generation toward omission or contradiction of verified EHR facts. Designing benchmarks that isolate such temporal influence effects remains an open challenge.

\paragraph{Multimodal Trust Anchoring and Integrity Checks.}
The trust-anchored defense evaluated in this work operates over textual summaries. Extending this approach to multimodal settings raises new questions: how should trusted and untrusted visual inputs be separated, summarized, and compared? What constitutes a semantic conflict between structured EHR facts and visual evidence? Future systems may require modality-aware integrity checks that explicitly reason over trust provenance across text, images, and video, rather than assuming uniform reliability \citep{owasp_llm01}.

\paragraph{Human-in-the-Loop and Deployment Considerations.}
Finally, while our defense surfaces conflicts for clinician review, future work should explore how multimodal conflicts are best presented to clinicians in practice. Understanding cognitive load, alert fatigue, and interpretability of multimodal integrity violations will be essential for safe deployment.
