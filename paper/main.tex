\begin{abstract}
  Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into electronic health record (EHR) systems for clinical summarization, yet their security properties in realistic EHR workflows remain underexplored. Prior work evaluates LLM robustness largely in isolation, without systematically studying models operating over mixed-trust EHR inputs that combine verified clinical facts with untrusted patient-provided instructions. We introduce the first benchmark for evaluating generation-time semantic integrity of LLMs in an EHR system setting. Using synthetic patient records, we simulate a realistic EHR summarization pipeline and evaluate six widely used LLMs under clean and adversarial conditions. Focusing on safety-critical facts such as documented allergies, we find that adversarial patient instructions can reliably induce fact omission despite explicit access to ground-truth records: all six commonly used LLMs in modern EHR systems exhibit attack success rates near or above 90\%. We further evaluate a trust-anchoring defense that redundantly injects verified EHR facts into the generation context; while this defense does not prevent attacks, it significantly reduces silent failures by forcing conflicts to become explicit. These results demonstrate that current LLMs lack robust trust-boundary enforcement in EHR-integrated settings and motivate standardized benchmarks and defenses tailored to clinical deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electronic Health Record (EHR) systems store clinician-verified chart facts (e.g., diagnoses, medications, allergies, labs) alongside unstructured notes and patient-portal messages and support routine clinical workflows \citep{onc_ehr_benefits,onc_playbook_ehr,wang2023ehr_source}.

LLMs are increasingly integrated into EHR workflows for chart summarization and related documentation tasks \citep{woo2025scoping,bednarczyk2025summarization,aalijamia2025bhc}. In practice, the generation context can be \emph{mixed-trust}: verified chart facts are co-presented with untrusted patient-authored text. Even when the underlying database remains correct, untrusted claims can distort the generated summary by overriding or suppressing verified facts during generation.

We study a safety-critical failure mode specific to this integration: \emph{generation-time semantic integrity violations}, where clinician-verified facts are omitted or contradicted in the produced summary because the model mis-handles conflicting trusted and untrusted inputs. This mechanism is distinct from database tampering or system compromise and complements broader concerns about factual reliability of clinical LLM outputs \citep{chung2025nejmai,croxford2025evaluating}.

Prior clinical LLM benchmarks primarily emphasize task performance (e.g., QA accuracy or summarization quality) under a single trust assumption and do not explicitly test whether verified facts are preserved in the presence of untrusted instructions \citep{kweon2024ehrnoteqa,aalijamia2025bhc}. As a result, they provide limited insight into trust-boundary failures in realistic EHR-integrated settings.

In this work, we introduce a benchmark for evaluating semantic integrity in EHR-integrated LLMs under mixed-trust inputs. The benchmark formalizes a simple but realistic workflow: (1) the model is provided with structured ground-truth patient data representing clinician-verified EHR facts; (2) an untrusted instruction is introduced; (3) the model is tasked with generating a clinical summary; and (4) the output is compared against the ground truth to identify integrity violations. We consider both clean and adversarial conditions to isolate the effect of untrusted instruction dominance, closely related to prompt-injection--style vulnerabilities described in prior LLM security guidance \citep{owasp_prompt_injection}.

Using this framework, we evaluate multiple frontier and open-weight LLMs on allergy and medication summarization. We quantify integrity failures via omission- and contradiction-oriented metrics and analyze how vulnerability varies across models. We also evaluate a defense we implement, \emph{trust-anchored summarization}, which separates trusted chart facts from untrusted patient text at generation time and uses an integrity check to flag semantic conflicts before any untrusted content is incorporated into the clinician-facing summary; we treat this defense as a diagnostic mechanism rather than a complete mitigation.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We introduce a benchmark that explicitly models mixed-trust EHR workflows, enabling systematic evaluation of semantic integrity violations in LLM-generated clinical summaries.
    \item We show that untrusted instructions can induce clinically meaningful omissions and contradictions across multiple models, even when underlying EHR data remains correct.
    \item We demonstrate that enforcing explicit trust separation and integrity checks can reduce or expose integrity failures, transforming silent errors into inspectable violations.
\end{enumerate}

By isolating generation-time semantic integrity failures from infrastructure compromise and training-time attacks, this benchmark provides a focused perspective on a critical yet under-evaluated risk in real-world clinical LLM deployment.

\section{Background and Problem Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

Modern EHR systems combine clinician-verified structured fields with unstructured narrative content and patient-facing portal messages \citep{ehrenstein2019obtaining,kim2024challenges,onc_secure_messages,ahrq_secure_messaging}. In EHR-integrated LLM workflows, these sources may be assembled into a single prompt for summarization, creating an implicit trust boundary between verified chart facts and untrusted patient-authored text.

\subsection{Threat Model}
We consider an adversary who can submit \emph{untrusted free-text content} via a patient-facing channel (e.g., patient-portal secure messages or similar text that is ingested into the summarization context) that may be included verbatim alongside clinician-verified EHR facts.
Concretely, this capability can arise either because the attacker manipulates/tricks the patient into submitting the adversarial content, or because the attacker compromises/stole patient credentials and submits messages directly via the portal.
The attacker’s goal is to induce \emph{generation-time semantic integrity violations} in the provider-facing summary---specifically, omission or contradiction of safety-critical chart facts such as allergies and active medications.
The attacker may send a single message or multiple messages over time and can choose message timing within the normal workflow, but cannot directly modify structured EHR fields, clinician-entered problem lists, or the underlying database.
We assume no access to system prompts, no tool use, and no privileged retrieval beyond what the standard EHR interface places into the model context.
The system goal is to produce a concise chart summary that preserves clinician-verified facts even when untrusted text is present; failures matter because clinicians may rely on the summary during time-sensitive decision-making.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLMs for Clinical Documentation}

LLMs are increasingly explored for clinical documentation tasks including chart summarization and handoff-style notes \citep{woo2025scoping,bednarczyk2025summarization,aali2024mimicivbhc}. This literature primarily evaluates quality and coverage, and typically does not isolate failures that arise when untrusted patient-authored text is co-presented with clinician-verified chart facts in an EHR-integrated workflow. Related work also studies general-purpose versus specialized clinical models \citep{lehman2023does,singhal2023large}.

\subsection{Clinical Benchmarks for EHR-Centered Language Tasks}

Benchmarks for EHR-centered language tasks include QA over notes and structured records \citep{pampari2018emrqa,kweon2024ehrnoteqa} and broader evaluations of general-purpose LLMs on EHR-derived workflows \citep{liu2025medhelm}. These settings generally evaluate performance under a single trust assumption and do not directly test whether verified chart facts are preserved under mixed-trust inputs. Earlier clinical-domain models (e.g., ClinicalBERT-style encoders) support many EHR NLP tasks, but are not designed to enforce trust boundaries during instruction-following generation \citep{alsentzer2019publicly}.

\subsection{Factuality and Reliability of Clinical LLM Outputs}

Evaluations of clinical LLMs emphasize factuality, including hallucinations, omissions, and verification \citep{chung2025nejmai}. We complement this line of work by isolating an integration-driven mechanism: even when clinician-verified facts are explicitly provided, they can be omitted or contradicted when mixed with untrusted patient-authored text in the generation context.

\subsection{Prompt Injection and Mixed-Trust Inputs}

Prompt injection stems from weak separation between instructions and untrusted data \citep{owasp_prompt_injection}. While most prior work targets general-purpose or agentic settings, the underlying mechanism is directly relevant to EHR-integrated workflows where patient text and verified chart facts may be combined within a single prompt, enabling mixed-trust context injection that overrides verified facts during summarization.

Recent work demonstrates both direct prompt-injection attacks on LLMs and indirect instruction injection through integrated applications and retrieved content \citep{greshake2023not,bagdasaryan2023multimodal}.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

In contrast to prior clinical benchmarks, we explicitly model mixed-trust EHR workflows and evaluate semantic integrity under clean, adversarial, and defended conditions. Concretely, the mechanism is \emph{mixed-trust context injection} through the EHR integration layer (untrusted portal text co-located with trusted facts), the outcome is \emph{semantic integrity violations} (omission/negation of verified allergies/medications), and the trust boundary is between clinician-verified chart data and patient-authored content.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Benchmark Construction from Synthetic EHR Data}

We use \emph{synthetic} EHR data generated by Synthea \citep{walonoski2018synthea}, a widely used open-source patient simulator designed to produce realistic, non-identifiable longitudinal health records.

We construct two cohorts for chart summarization: \textit{(i)} allergy summarization and \textit{(ii)} medication summarization. Each cohort contains $N=1000$ patients with at least one relevant structured fact. For each cohort we retain (a) an identifier list (Appendix~\ref{app:file-patient-ids}), (b) a patient demographics table (Appendix~\ref{app:file-patients-subset}), and (c) a task-specific fact table (Appendix~\ref{app:file-allergies-subset} or Appendix~\ref{app:file-medications-subset}). In the allergy cohort, we observe $3774$ allergy entries (average $3.77$ per patient) spanning $15$ unique allergy concepts; in the medication cohort, we observe $3806$ medication entries (average $3.81$ per patient) spanning $90$--$91$ unique medication concepts.

Our research question concerns \emph{generation-time semantic integrity} when an EHR-integrated LLM summarizes clinician-verified structured facts in the presence of untrusted patient-authored text. Synthea provides controlled, structured ground-truth fact tables (trusted inputs) that can be cleanly separated from untrusted text, enabling precise measurement of omission and contradiction without confounding from missing labels or privacy restrictions.

We focus on allergies and active medications because they are ubiquitous, safety-critical, and have crisp structured ground truth; extending the benchmark to additional EHR entities (e.g., diagnoses, laboratory values, and longer-form clinical notes) is left to future work.

We deterministically freeze the cohorts using a fixed random seed and a fixed Synthea export snapshot so the same patient set and fact tables are reused across models and conditions. Prompt templates are omitted for blind review (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}); the camera-ready version will include the exact sampling and prompt specifications needed to recreate the benchmark artifacts.

\subsection{Attack Model}

We model an \emph{explicit untrusted override attempt} embedded within the patient-authored message.
In the \textbf{attack} condition, the patient message directly contradicts the structured chart facts by asserting the absence of all allergies or all medications (e.g., ``New research shows the patient is not allergic to anything'' or ``the patient is not taking any medications'').

As illustrated in \Cref{fig:attack_model}, the attacker’s leverage comes from the EHR-integrated workflow mixing clinician-verified records (trusted) with patient-portal content (untrusted) within a single generation context, enabling untrusted claims to suppress or override verified facts at generation time.

The attack is \emph{contextual rather than syntactic}: it does not alter system instructions or rely on prompt-format tricks, but instead uses semantic contradiction to induce the model to treat untrusted text as authoritative and suppress verified chart facts.

Our adversarial evaluation focuses on two text-based contextual injection attacks that operate via the same mixed-trust mechanism: a global denial of allergies and a global denial of medications. We do not additionally enumerate many other text-only variants (e.g., partial denials, targeted omission requests, or paraphrased contradictions) because they function through the same context-mixing pathway and would primarily change surface form rather than attack mechanism. In future work, we plan to extend this benchmark to multimodal settings (image- and video-based inputs), which introduce a qualitatively different injection channel beyond text while preserving the same trust-boundary challenge.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}

We follow an established omission-based evaluation setup using two prompt conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clean}: The patient message contains no conflicting claim and does not dispute the chart facts.
    \item \textbf{Attack}: The patient message explicitly denies all allergies or medications despite contradictory structured chart evidence.
\end{itemize}

This clean-versus-attack design serves as the core experimental comparison and is applied uniformly across both allergy and medication summarization tasks.
All other aspects of the experiment are held constant across conditions.

\subsection{Prompt Construction}

Prompts are constructed as a fixed message sequence containing (i) the task instruction, (ii) structured chart facts (one item per line), and (iii) a patient-authored message (clean vs.\ attack).
Model-specific prompt \emph{wrappers} are embedded directly to keep model runners as pure input--output interfaces.
To reduce causal ambiguity in cross-model comparisons, we restrict these differences to minimal formatting/stability tokens required by specific APIs (e.g., optional system preambles or ``no \texttt{<think>}'' directives) and do not change the task objective or threat semantics: across all models, the task instruction and structured facts are identical, the clean condition contains no conflicting claim, and the attack condition contains an explicit denial of all allergies/medications (up to paraphrase).
Exact prompt templates are omitted for blind review; see Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} for a placeholder description.
This design isolates model behavior under conflicting information without introducing confounding effects from tool invocation or external retrieval, but we note that any remaining prompt-level variation is a potential limitation when attributing differences solely to the model.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Model outputs are evaluated using automatic string matching against ground-truth chart facts.
For both allergy and medication tasks, we report:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recall}: the fraction of chart-ground-truth items mentioned in the generated summary.
    \item \textbf{Conflict rate}: under the defense, the fraction of cases flagged by the integrity check as containing a conflict between trusted chart facts and untrusted claims.
    \item \textbf{False-none rate}: the proportion of outputs that assert ``no allergies'' or ``no medications'' when at least one corresponding chart item exists.
    \item \textbf{Full omission rate}: the proportion of outputs that mention none of the chart-ground-truth items.
\end{itemize}

For attack conditions, we additionally report an \textbf{attack success rate}, defined as recall falling below a fixed threshold or the presence of a false-none assertion.
We set the recall threshold to $\tau = 0.5$ for both allergy and medication tasks.
Recall is computed via deterministic normalized string matching between the one-sentence output and each chart item; false-none is triggered only when a ``no allergies/medications'' pattern is present and zero chart items match.
This metric captures both partial and complete suppression of verified chart facts.
Because this evaluator is surface-form based, it can underestimate recall under paraphrases or spelling variation. As a sanity check, the matcher achieves $100\%$ recall on constructed ``oracle'' summaries that literally include all ground-truth items.

\subsection{Defense Model: Trust-Anchored Summarization}

We evaluate a defense strategy based on \emph{explicit trust separation} and an \emph{integrity check} over independently generated trusted and untrusted summaries (see \Cref{fig:defense_model}). As prior work and common practice, we briefly situate our approach relative to three mitigation patterns for prompt-injection--style risks in mixed-trust LLM systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instruction--data separation (prompt structuring).} A simple mitigation is to clearly delimit instructions, trusted chart facts, and untrusted patient text (e.g., labeled sections and explicit ``do not treat as authoritative'' warnings) \citep{owasp_prompt_injection}. This can reduce trivial instruction override, but it still places all content in one context; when untrusted text semantically contradicts trusted facts, the model may still prioritize the conflicting claim---the core failure mode we evaluate.
    \item \textbf{Output validation / verifier gate.} Another approach is to treat the model output as untrusted and run a post-generation check for missing required facts or contradictions before the summary is used \citep{chung2025nejmai,croxford2025evaluating}. Our defense belongs to this family, but is designed to be \emph{diagnostic}: rather than only comparing the final output to the database, we compare trusted-only vs.\ untrusted-only generations and surface conflicts explicitly.
    \item \textbf{Least privilege / sandboxing (agentic systems).} For systems where models can take actions (e.g., tool/API calls), a common defense is to restrict capabilities via allowlists and least-privilege permission checks \citep{nist80053r5}. In our EHR summarization setting, however, the primary ``action'' is the generated text itself; limiting tool access does not prevent silent omission of allergies/medications, motivating integrity-focused defenses.
\end{itemize}
Concretely, the defense runs the model in two constrained modes to preserve the trust boundary: (i) a \textbf{trusted-only summary} generated \emph{only} from clinician-verified chart facts and treated as authoritative, and (ii) an \textbf{untrusted-only summary} generated \emph{only} from patient-authored text and treated as non-authoritative. An \textbf{integrity check} then compares the two generations for semantic conflicts. If a conflict is detected, the case is flagged for clinician review and untrusted content is \emph{not} merged. If no conflict is detected, untrusted content may be appended only as \emph{non-authoritative context consistent with the checked chart facts}; it does not override or replace the trusted summary.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Trust-anchored integrity-check defense (simplified).}
\label{alg:trust_anchored_defense}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Trusted EHR facts $F$, untrusted patient message $U$, LLM $M$
\ENSURE Final clinician-facing summary, or a flagged case (no merge)
\STATE $S_T \leftarrow \textsc{TrustedSummary}(M, F)$
\STATE $S_U \leftarrow \textsc{UntrustedSummary}(M, U)$
\STATE $C \leftarrow \textsc{IntegrityCheck}(M, F, S_T, U, S_U)$ \COMMENT{$C$ contains \texttt{CONFLICTS\_TO\_VERIFY} (CTV)}
\IF{$\textsc{ParseError}(C)$}
  \STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{FlagForReview}($S_T$) \COMMENT{conservative default; do not merge}
\ENDIF
\STATE $\mathit{conflicts} \leftarrow \textsc{ExtractCTVOrNONE}(C)$
\IF{$\mathit{conflicts} \neq \text{NONE}$}
  \STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{FlagForReview}($S_T$, $\mathit{conflicts}$) \COMMENT{do not merge}
\ENDIF
\STATE \textbf{return} \textsc{MergeAsContext}($S_T$, $S_U$) \COMMENT{trusted facts take precedence}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Integrity Check Implementation}
In our implementation, the integrity check is rule-based and operates on an \emph{inspectable output format} enforced by the defense prompt: the model must emit labeled sections \texttt{SUMMARY\_TRUSTED}, \texttt{UNTRUSTED\_NOTE}, and a bullet list under \texttt{CONFLICTS\_TO\_VERIFY}.
We flag a conflict if \texttt{CONFLICTS\_TO\_VERIFY} contains any non-empty bullet other than normalized variants of ``none''/``no conflicts.'' 
If the expected headers or bullets are missing (formatting mismatch / parse error), we conservatively treat the case as a failure of the defense pipeline in our evaluation (and count it as an attack success under attack).
This checker does not run separate entailment or LLM-as-judge inference; instead, it measures whether the model explicitly surfaced a conflict in the designated section (including hedged/uncertain phrasing, if present).

See Appendix~\ref{app:prompts} for placeholder descriptions of the prompt templates and checker I/O format.

We evaluate this defense under clean and attack conditions, measuring (a) whether trusted EHR facts are preserved (fact retention / recall) and (b) whether conflicts are explicitly detected and surfaced.
This defense is intended to reduce \emph{silent} integrity failures in EHR-integrated LLM systems by making trust boundaries and disagreement explicit at generation time, rather than assuming a single mixed-trust context can be safely summarized.

The omission attack is consistently damaging in the baseline setting: several models exhibit near-complete failure under attack (e.g., allergy ASR up to $1.000$ and medication ASR up to $0.997$), showing that untrusted patient text can override clinician-verified chart facts during generation.

\textbf{Defense effectiveness.} The trust-anchored integrity-check defense reduces \emph{silent} integrity failures by surfacing conflicts, driving defended ASR close to zero on allergy omission (0.000--0.024) and materially lower on medication omission (0.049--0.198). Notably, defended ASR is consistently lower for allergy than for medication in our runs (allergy: 0.000--0.024 vs.\ medication: 0.049--0.198), indicating that medication omission yields more residual silent failures even when conflicts are surfaced.

\textbf{Standard defense baselines (IDS, sandboxing, verifier gate).}
We additionally implement three common prompt-injection mitigations discussed in \S\ref{sec:setting}--\S\ref{sec:results}: \emph{instruction--data separation} (IDS prompt structuring), \emph{least privilege / sandboxing} (exclude untrusted text), and an \emph{output validation / verifier gate} (post-generation check).
On reruns with $N=100$ patients for each task (clean + attack per patient; $200$ generations per model), \Cref{tab:std_defenses_combined} shows that IDS and sandboxing reduce ASR relative to baseline but do not eliminate failures entirely (e.g., allergy IDS ASR remains $0.26$ for \texttt{gpt51}). The verifier gate flags many attacked outputs, with detection varying by model (e.g., medication \texttt{llama33} flags $0.52$ of attacks vs.\ $1.00$ for several models) and a small clean-set false-flag rate ($0.01$--$0.09$).
This highlights an explicit detection trade-off in our data: models with very high attack-flag rates can still incur non-trivial clean false-flag rates (e.g., medication \texttt{claude45} flags $1.00$ of attacks but also flags $0.09$ under clean), while other models have lower clean false flags but weaker detection (e.g., medication \texttt{llama33}: $0.52$ attack flags, $0.01$ clean flags).
\textbf{Comparison to standard prompt-injection defenses.} Across our runs, the three standard defenses and our trust-anchored integrity-check defense emphasize different objectives, which are reflected in the reported metrics. IDS and sandboxing are \emph{preventive} (lower ASR under attack in \Cref{tab:std_defenses_combined}), but still permit non-zero attack success in some model/task pairs (e.g., medication sandbox ASR $=0.21$ for \texttt{llama31} and allergy IDS ASR $=0.26$ for \texttt{gpt51}). The verifier gate is \emph{detective}: it can flag a large fraction of attacked outputs (often $\ge 0.80$; up to $1.00$), but this comes with an explicit false-positive rate under clean inputs ($0.01$--$0.09$). Our trust-anchored integrity-check defense is \emph{diagnostic} in the sense that it targets the residual rate of \emph{silent} failures under attack (\Cref{tab:asr}): it drives silent ASR near zero for allergy (0.000--0.024) and substantially lower for medication (0.049--0.198), while maintaining high clean trusted-summary correctness in our defended runs (allergy: 0.976--1.000; medication: 0.799--0.926). These comparisons use different evaluation sets ($N=1000$ for \Cref{tab:asr} vs.\ $N=100$ reruns for \Cref{tab:std_defenses_combined}), so they should be interpreted as complementary perspectives rather than a single head-to-head ranking.

\textbf{Clean-set costs and failure modes (limitations).}
While the defense is effective at surfacing attacks, it can introduce practical costs.
In this batch, \texttt{llama31} exhibits extremely high conflict-flag rates even on clean inputs (clean \texttt{conflict\_flagged\_rate} $=0.992$ for allergy and $=1.000$ for medication), and \texttt{claude45} on medication shows elevated parse errors (clean \texttt{parse\_error\_rate} $=0.117$) alongside the highest defended ASR, indicating reduced stability in that setting.
See Appendix~\Cref{app:file-experiment-overview} for the consolidated per-model clean-set stability/cost export used to summarize these indicators.

\textbf{Mitigating clean-set costs (guidance).}
To reduce spurious conflicts and parse errors in deployment, practitioners can enforce stricter structured outputs, apply lightweight retry/repair on formatting failures, and calibrate conflict sensitivity to reduce false flags and alert fatigue.

\textbf{Sensitivity note.}
Absolute ASR values may be sensitive to prompt formatting and message placement (e.g., wrapper conventions and ordering/recency effects); we treat the results as representative for the evaluated templates.

\textbf{Scope note (multimodal scenarios).}
Our current benchmark evaluates \emph{textual} mixed-trust context injection because patient-portal messages and other free-text inputs are a common, high-leverage untrusted channel in EHR workflows, and they let us measure generation-time integrity against structured ground truth with minimal confounds. We do not include pilot experiments for image/video-based attacks in the present artifact set; extending to multimodal inputs would require specifying additional trusted-versus-untrusted modalities (e.g., scanned documents or images attached to messages), defining modality-appropriate ground truth and evaluators, and controlling for OCR/vision extraction errors. We therefore treat multimodal injection as an explicit future-work axis rather than a claim evaluated in this paper.

\section{Future Work}

Future work includes targeted human validation, evaluation of domain-adapted clinical LLMs, extension to additional safety-critical EHR entities (e.g., diagnoses and labs), and more robust conflict detection beyond rule-based parsing. We also plan to extend the benchmark beyond text to multimodal inputs (images/video), introducing new injection channels under the same mixed-trust trust-boundary setting.

\section{Impact Statement}
This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning for safer EHR-integrated summarization. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none of which we feel must be specifically highlighted here beyond the well-established clinical AI safety considerations around reliability, human oversight, and responsible deployment.
